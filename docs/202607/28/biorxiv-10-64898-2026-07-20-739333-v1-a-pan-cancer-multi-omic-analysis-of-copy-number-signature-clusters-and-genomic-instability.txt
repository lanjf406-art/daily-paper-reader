# A Pan-Cancer Multi-Omic Analysis of Copy Number Signature Clusters and Genomic Instability 

Marco Rota Negroni 1 , Ilaria Billato 1 , Chiara Romualdi 1* 1 Department of Biology, University of Padova, via U. Bassi 58/B, 35121 Padova, Italy * Corresponding author: chiara.romualdi@unipd.it Keywords: Copy number signatures; genomic instability; pan-cancer analysis; multi-omics; machine learning; survival analysis 

# Abstract 

Copy number alterations (CNAs) are major contributors to genomic instability in cancer, and copy number signatures (CNS) provide a compact representation of the processes shaping CNA landscapes. However, the relationships among existing CNS frameworks and their predictability from molecular data other than whole-genome sequencing remain unclear. Here, we compare three major CNS compendia across more than 5,800 TCGA cancer samples, evaluating their overlap, complementarity, biological relevance, and prognostic associations. Individual signatures showed limited cross-study concordance, whereas signature-derived clusters identified biologically distinct patient groups, including favorable-outcome clusters observed across all frameworks. Using gene expression, DNA methylation, somatic mutation features, age, and tumor purity, XGBoost models predicted cluster membership with framework-dependent performance, achieving high F1-scores for the Drews and Steele compendia but limited performance for Tao. Feature importance analysis highlighted expression-driven predictors and pathways linked to genomic instability. These findings show that current CNS frameworks capture complementary rather than interchangeable dimensions of tumor genome instability and suggest that multi-omic profiles can extend signature-based stratification to cohorts without whole-genome sequencing. 

# Introduction 

Copy number alterations (CNAs) are genomic changes where segments of DNA are deleted or amplified, leading to an abnormal number of copies of certain genes or genomic regions. They can range from small, focal changes affecting single genes to large-scale chromosomal alterations. CNAs play a critical role in various diseases, especially in cancer activating oncogenes or inactivating tumor suppressor genes and promoting genomic instability, leading to further mutations and tumor progression. In non-cancerous conditions, CNAs are associated with developmental disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and immune system dysfunctions. An important piece of work recently has been dedicated to the identification of Copy Number Signatures (CNS) [1,2,3], patterns of CNAs across the genome that reflect the underlying biological processes

It has been shown that these signatures can be used to i) infer the mechanisms of genomic instability (such as defective DNA repair pathways or chromothripsis) [1], ii) classify tumor subtypes, or iii) predict clinical outcomes (such as treatment response, or resistance to therapies) [2, 3]. The most widely used method for inferring copy number signatures is Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF), valued for its flexibility and the interpretability of its results. This approach has been predominantly applied to data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), enabling the identification of pan-cancer CNS [1,2,3]. To date, three studies on CNS identification have been published, collectively identifying fifty-eight distinct signatures, two of them published simultaneously in 2022. Drews et al. [1] presents a compendium of 17 CNS characterizing specific types of genomic instability, with their putative aetiologies with some of them able to predict drug response and to identify new drug targets. A recent follow-up further demonstrated the clinical utility of Drews chromosomal instability signatures by validating their predictive power for chemotherapy resistance through emulated biomarker clinical trials across multiple cancer types [4]. Steele et al [2] presents a set of 21 CNS that explain the copy number patterns of 97% of samples. Seventeen CNS were attributed to biological phenomena of whole-genome doubling, aneuploidy, loss of heterozygosity, homologous recombination deficiency, chromothripsis and haploidization while four CNS remain unexplained. One year later, in 2023, Tao and colleagues [3] on the same data but with a slightly different mechanism-agnostic approach, identified 20 CNS some of which were associated with known biological characteristics and patients' prognosis. Drews, Steele and Tao all decompose copy-number profiles into signatures, but they capture different dimensions of genomic instability. Drews et al. focus on chromosomal instability using breakpoint density, segment size, copy-number shifts and oscillating patterns, thereby emphasizing specific CIN mechanisms, including distinct HRD-related processes. Steele et al. encode total copy number, LOH status and segment size, making their framework particularly suited to capture broad genomic states and macro-evolutionary processes such as WGD, aneuploidy, chromothripsis, focal LOH and chromosomal-scale LOH. Tao et al. extend this concept by incorporating absolute copy number, LOH, segment size and local copy-number context, providing a granular representation of CNA morphology across PCAWG WGS and TCGA SNP-array datasets (Figure 1B). These methodological differences are important when CNA signatures are used as predictors of genomic instability. A signature associated with instability in one framework may represent a specific CIN mechanism, a global ploidy state, or a composite CNA morphology in another. Consequently, signatures derived from these approaches could in some cases provide complementary representations: Drews' signatures are most informative for mechanistic CIN and HRD-related instability, Steele's for broad genomic states and macro-evolutionary events, and Tao's for generalized CNA morphology and cross-dataset pattern recognition. In this perspective, no systematic comparative analyses have yet been conducted to evaluate their degree of interchangeability, nor to assess the robustness of the underlying results. More importantly, no efforts have been made to investigate whether these signatures can be predicted using molecular data other than Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS), such as gene expression, DNA methylation, or somatic mutation profiles - an important consideration given the high cost of WGS, particularly in large-scale studies. In this study, we aim to address these gaps by thoroughly exploring the relationships among existing copy number signatures, assessing their similarity and complementarity, and leveraging machine learning

# Results 

To maintain clarity in both the text and figures, the three signature compendia will be referred to using the first author's name: Drews, Steele, and Tao signatures. Additionally, the code identifiers for each signature will adhere to the original nomenclature: CX for Drews, CN for Steele, and Sig for Tao. 

Signature Activity Distribution Across Studies 

The total tumour sample size profiled by CNV signature is 6,335 for Drews, 9,699 for Steele, and 10,370 for Tao, distributed across 33 cancer types, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Unless otherwise specified, analyses were conducted using these sample sizes. For comparisons across studies, however, we utilized the 5,881 samples common to all three datasets. The activity of each signature is quantified on a scale from 0 (inactive) to 1 (highly active). Figure 2a-c shows the distribution of activity values for each signature across all samples, regardless of cancer type. We can observe that approximately most of the signatures tend to have low activation or to be inactive. Only a few signatures exhibit significant activation levels, in Drews (CX1, CX2, CX3, CX5) and Steele (CN1, CN2, CN9, CN17), while the Tao signatures are characterized by generally low but uniform activation levels, with slightly higher activity observed for Sig1, Sig2, and Sig5, Sig7. 

Exploring Sources of Variability 

The TCGA data originate from multiple centers and diverse technologies, making batch effects a likely concern in this context. Before conducting downstream analyses, we examined the variability of the signatures and assessed whether they captured any spurious variability using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). As shown in Figure 2d-f, PCA revealed distinct patterns of signature contributions across the three studies that reflect their activity levels as discussed in the previous section. In the Drews and Steele studies, four dominant signatures were identified (CX1, CX2, CX3, and CX5 in Drews; CN1, CN2, CN9, and CN17 in Steele), with minimal contributions from the other signatures to the overall variance. In contrast, the Tao study displayed a different pattern, with three signatures (Sig1, Sig2, and Sig5) exhibiting strong contributions, while the remaining signatures contributed moderately and uniformly to the variance but in the opposite direction. To explore potential sources of variability, we considered both technical and biological factors. Technical sources included tissue source site (TSS code), geographical location (city, state, and country), and biospecimen core resources (BCR). Biological factors, such as tumor type, were also evaluated. From exploratory analyses only tumor type and TSS code exhibited patterns warranting further investigation. To rigorously assess their effects, we applied linear regression to the first five principal components (PCs), using tumor type and TSS code as covariates. The results, summarized in Figure 2g-l, present the R-squared values of the regression models for TSS (Figure 2g-i) and tumor type (Figure 2j-l) across the three studies. As expected, tumor type emerged as the covariate with the highest (but moderate R2=0.3) R-squared values, particularly in the Drews study, highlighting moderate biological differences in genomic instability mechanisms across specific tumor types. Patient Clustering Reveals Distinct Patterns of Signature Activation 

Rather than analyzing each signature in isolation, we aimed to obtain a global view of signature activity patterns across all samples. To this end, we performed clustering analysis (Figure 5a, d, g) to assess patient stratification based on the set of active signatures. The selection of the number of clusters for each dataset was based on the approach proposed by Zappia and Oshlack [5] (see Methods for details and Supplementary Figure 2). The Drews signatures (Figure 5a) separated samples into four distinct clusters, primarily driven by the combined activation patterns of CX1, CX2, CX3, and CX5. Similarly, the Steele signatures (Figure 5d) identified four clusters, each associated with specific dominant signatures (CN1, CN2, CN9, CN17). In contrast, the Tao signatures (Figure 5g) resulted in a stratification with three clusters. Figure 5b, e, h shows the distribution of signature activity values within the major clusters for each dataset. In the Drews dataset (Figure 5b), Cluster 2 is marked by high CX1 activation and complete inactivation of CX2, CX3, and CX5, suggesting intact homologous recombination repair. Cluster 4 shows a similar trend but with lower CX1 levels and partial activation of CX2 and CX3, indicating increasing HR deficiency. Cluster 3 is characterized by moderate activation of CX1 and CX3, which may reflect the presence of large genomic rearrangements impairing HR. Cluster 1 shows moderate activation across all active signatures. In the Steele dataset (Figure 5e), Cluster 1 exhibits high CN9 activity, a signature of chromosomal instability in a diploid context with LOH and moderate CN1. Cluster 2 is enriched in CN1, indicative of a stable diploid genome. Cluster 3 is defined by high CN2 activity (associated with tetraploidy), while Cluster 4 shows CN17 activity (linked to HR deficiency) along with moderate CN2 activation. In the Tao dataset (Figure 5h), Clusters 1 and 3 are characterized by high and moderate levels of Sig 7 (associated to whole genome doubling), respectively, whereas Cluster 2 displays strong activation of Signatures 1, 2, and 5 (stable genome with shallow modifications), with a notable absence of Signature 7. The distribution of tumor types across these clusters (Figure 5c, f, i) reveals a quite heterogeneous pattern: while some cancer types tend to localize within specific clusters, others are more evenly distributed. 

Copy Number Signature Clusters as Prognostic Markers Across Cancer Types 

The original studies investigated the association between copy number signatures and clinical outcomes. Despite using different models and methodologies, all three studies identified specific signatures linked to prognosis in particular tumor types. Here, we extended this analysis by performing a survival analysis of the signature-based clusters identified in the previous section. Kaplan-Meier curves are shown in Figure 6, with corresponding risk tables in Supplementary Figure 5. Consistent with the tumor-type composition shown in Figure 5c, f, i, signature-defined clusters were not randomly distributed across cancer types. For example, the favorable Drews Cluster 2 was enriched for renal, endocrine/neuroendocrine, brain/nervous system tumors and LAML, whereas poor-prognosis Steele clusters included gastrointestinal and thoracic tumors. Age and sample purity were retained as continuous adjustment covariates. Figure 7 summarizes the stratified Cox results, which support a residual pan-cancer prognostic association of Cluster 2 after stratification by tumor type. Thus, the prognostic signal is directionally consistent and remains significant in five of six compendium-endpoint comparisons, but its magnitude varies across signature frameworks. These results indicate that cluster assignment captures prognostic information beyond tumor-type composition, while also suggesting that the effect is moderate and not uniformly detectable across all endpoints. We then asked whether the Cluster 2 classifications obtained from each signature set identified the same patient populations, or whether different signature types captured distinct biological features. As shown in Supplementary Figure 3a-b, only a small proportion of samples overlap across all three Cluster 2 groups, with the strongest concordance observed between Steele and Tao. This limited overlap suggests that each signature type captures unique and complementary aspects of tumor biology. Overall, these results suggest that genome-instability patterns captured by copy number signature clusters are associated with tumour aggressiveness. The partial overlap between Drews-defined clusters and those derived from Steele and Tao further indicates that the three frameworks capture distinct yet complementary aspects of genomic instability. 

Multi-Omics Machine Learning Predicts Copy Number Signature Clusters 

Genomic instability can result from various molecular mechanisms, including defects in DNA repair pathways and alterations in DNA methylation. Conversely, it can also influence gene expression, as changes in gene copy number may reshape transcriptional regulatory networks. Based on this premise, we investigated whether copy number signatures and their derived clusters are associated with specific molecular features. This analysis had two main goals: first, to characterize these associations and better understand their biological relevance; and second, to assess whether copy number signatures could be predicted using molecular data other than Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS). This is particularly relevant given the high cost of WGS, especially in large-scale studies. We used TCGA-derived molecular features - gene expression, methylation, and mutation data -preprocessed as described in the Methods section.

Deep learning was selected for its ability to capture complex non-linear relationships in high-dimensional, sparse datasets - a characteristic of copy number signature profiles, as shown in our previous analyses. While some signatures proved more predictable than others (CX1, CX2, CN1, Sig2 Sig5 and CX5 Supplementary Figure 4), the R² values were generally modest, with a maximum of 0.65 for CX1 using Ridge regression. Among the tested methods, XGBoost and Ridge performed best, with XGBoost showing particular strength for skewed distributions, such as those seen in the Steele and Tao signatures. In contrast, predictive performance improved substantially when modeling signature-based clusters. We selected XGBoost for this task, given its superior performance, particularly with skewed data, and focused on predicting the two clusters with the most divergent survival outcomes (Cluster 1 vs Cluster 2) in each dataset. Given the imbalance of the two classes we decided to use the F1-score to compare model performance. F1 considers both precision and recall, making it particularly suitable for identifying patients in the less frequent but clinically relevant cluster. The F1-scores for the test set were remarkably high for Drews (F1=0.93) and for Steele (F1=0.80), but notably low for Tao (F1=0.24). To better contextualize these results, we examined performance by tumor type (Figure 8), and reported accuracy instead of F1-score, as the substantial imbalance in sample sizes across tumor types makes accuracy a more interpretable measure of per-group performance. This analysis showed that most tumor types achieved classification accuracies above 75%. Among the best-performing tumor types were gynecological cancers (UCS, OV, UCEC) with high chromosomal instability, endocrine tumors (THCA, PCPG) and KICH with stable genomes, LUSC and LIHC with extensive aneuploidy and WGD, and UVM with its distinctive low-mutation high-CNV profile. Taken together, our results indicate that clusters derived by signature patterns-particularly those associated with divergent prognoses-can be accurately predicted using multi-omic data without relying on whole-genome sequencing, achieving classification F1-score up to 93%. Importantly, tumor type was not included as a predictor in any of the models. The feature matrix comprised exclusively molecular omic features - gene expression, DNA methylation, and mutation data - without any cancer type label. This design ensures that the high classification performance reflects learned associations with the underlying biology of copy number instability and cannot be attributed to implicit tissue-of-origin prediction.

Performing enrichment analysis we found that only the Drews model's feature list was significantly enriched in specific Reactome pathways (Figure 8f), several of which are tightly linked to genomic instability. Several of these pathways are mechanistically linked to genomic instability: Oxidative Stress Induced Senescence and ERBB2 signaling both involve replication stress and ROS-driven DNA damage; NOTCH signaling has been linked to impaired DNA repair and deregulated cell cycle progression; mitotic stress (defects in spindle assembly, centrosome duplication, or checkpoint failure) directly promotes chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy. In the Steele model-and especially in the Tao model-tumor purity (the proportion of malignant cells relative to non-tumor components, such as immune, stromal, or endothelial cells) also emerged as a key predictive feature (Figure 8e, Supplementary Data 1). The relevance of tumor purity in these models is consistent with our earlier survival analyses, where it was significantly associated with progression-free interval (PFI) in both the Steele and Tao cohorts (Supplementary Table 2). This issue does not arise in the Drews signatures, as the authors pre-selected only high-purity samples (purity > 0.4). Biologically, low purity reflects greater infiltration by the tumor microenvironment (TME), typically associated with increased immune and stromal content. These features are known to influence both prognosis and response to therapy. However, from a technical perspective, low purity may dilute tumor-specific signals-particularly those related to genomic instability-leading to an underestimation of molecular alterations and a possible misclassification of tumors as genomically stable. Finally, to enhance the translational relevance of our approach, we evaluated the predictive performance of the model using only gene expression data-the omic layer that not only emerged as the most informative in our multi-omic framework, but is also the most widely available in public repositories and literature. Remarkably, the model retained high predictive power even in this reduced setting, achieving an F1-score of 0.94 in the Drews dataset, 0.78 in the Steele dataset, and 0.30 in the Tao dataset. 

# Discussion 

This study provides a systematic pan-cancer comparison of existing copy number signature models, highlighting both shared and framework-specific representations of genomic instability. By focusing on the most active signatures, we show that patients can be stratified into groups with distinct instability patterns. Although the resulting clusters overlap only partially across the Drews, Steele, and Tao compendia, all three frameworks identify a favorable-outcome cluster, supporting the biological relevance of signature-derived patient stratification. The results also extend previous work by showing that copy number signature-derived clusters can be predicted from molecular data types other than whole-genome sequencing, including gene expression,

The main limitation of this study is the absence of an independent external validation cohort. Replicating the full analytical framework requires matched genome-wide copy number profiles for signature calling across all three compendia, gene expression, DNA methylation, somatic mutation data, and clinical outcome information. To our knowledge, no public pan-cancer resource currently provides this combination at TCGA scale. Disease-specific cohorts such as METABRIC provide rich molecular and clinical information but lack the tumor-type diversity required for pan-cancer validation, whereas broader resources such as ICGC do not provide uniformly matched data layers across samples. Future prospective multi-omic pan-cancer cohorts will therefore be essential to validate the generalizability and translational utility of these models. 

# Methods 

Statistical analyses 

Principal Component Analyses were performed using the prcomp function in R. To assess the impact of TSS and tumor type, regression models were applied using the lm function in R. Correlation plots were generated with the cor function from the stats package for Pearson correlation. Hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted using complete linkage and Euclidean distance. Survival analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models, as implemented in the coxph function from the survival package, while Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the survfit function. To evaluate whether the prognostic association of signature clusters persisted after accounting for tumor-type-specific baseline hazards, stratified Cox proportional hazards models were fitted using the coxph function (survival package). Models included cluster membership (as factor), age, and tumor purity as covariates, with tumor type as a stratification variable (strata(cancer_type)), allowing the baseline hazard to vary across cancer types while estimating a single common cluster effect. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free interval (PFI) were analyzed separately for each compendium. Continuous signature variables were dichotomized at the maximal log-rank cutpoint using the surv_cutpoint function (survminer package). As we observed a high degree of instability in the clustering results across different resolution settings, we chose to determine the number of clusters using the approach proposed by Zappia and Oshlack [5]. This method systematically explores the effects of varying clustering resolution, revealing the relationships between clusters at multiple levels. It allows researchers to track how samples shift across resolutions and to visualize how clusters merge or split as the granularity increases, identifying the most stable and biologically meaningful resolution for each dataset.

In the first, individual copy number signature activities (continuous values, range 0-1) were predicted as the outcome variable (Y) using penalized regression (Lasso and Ridge), Support Vector Machines (SVM, linear and radial kernels), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), implemented via the caret package in R, alongside a fully connected deep

In the second task - the primary focus of this study -binary cluster membership (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2, the two clusters with the most divergent prognosis) was used as the outcome variable (Y) and predicted using XGBoost. In both tasks, the predictor matrix (X) consisted of molecular omic features: gene expression values (12,653 genes), DNA methylation beta-values (9,227 CpG sites), mutation pathway scores (13,612 features), age, and tumor purity. Tumor type was explicitly excluded from all predictor matrices, whereas age and tumor purity were included as additional numeric features. This design prevents direct tissue-of-origin prediction while allowing the models to account for clinical and sample-level variables that may influence molecular signal quality. An 80-20 train-test split was applied with tumor-type-stratified sampling to ensure proportional representation across cancer types in both partitions. Training incorporated 10-fold cross-validation and model-specific hyperparameter tuning. Classification performance was evaluated using the F1-score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), chosen for its robustness to class imbalance between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. Regression performance for individual signature prediction was assessed using MSE, MAE, and R². Before proceeding with the predictive analyses, a series of filtering steps was necessary, as detailed in the following section. 

Data preprocessing 

Starting from the samples with available signature estimates, we retained only those with both gene expression and methylation data. We then filtered out low-variance features (bottom 20% based on the coefficient of variation) and removed highly correlated features (|correlation| > 0.90). Age and tumor purity were included as additional numeric features in the predictor matrix. Tumor type was retained in the dataset for stratified train-test splitting but was not included as a predictor in any model. For mutation data, we again retained only samples with both gene expression and methylation profiles. Silent mutations were excluded, and dimensionality was reduced by grouping genes into mutational pathways, following the approach described in [9] and using KEGG pathway annotations available through the KEGGREST and org.Hs.eg.db R packages. Genes lacking pathway annotations were included as individual variables. The final preprocessed dataset comprises over 35,000 features, including 12,653 gene expression values, 9,227 methylation features, 13,340 mutation-level features, and 272 pathway-based mutation annotations. While mutation and expression data showed no significant batch effects, methylation data show an evident platform-specific effect. Details of the procedure to eliminate this effect are reported in the next section. 

Batch effect in methylation data 

Methylation data showed significant platform-specific effects: TCGA samples were profiled on either 27k or 450k arrays, with substantial batch effects driven by differences in CpG coverage (confirmed using LAML, the only tumor type with both platforms). Additionally, the 450k platform shows internal bias due to two probe types (Infinium I and II) with different dynamic ranges [10,11]. To address these issues, we implemented a harmonization workflow combining the minfi package [13] and BMIQ normalization [14]: separate normalization of 27k and 450k data, mapping of 27k CpGs to corresponding 450k positions, removal of SNP-containing probes, and selection of a common CpG set across arrays.
